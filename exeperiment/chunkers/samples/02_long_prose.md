# Reviewing a long narrative

## A single continuous section

A useful review starts with a question rather than a conclusion. The reviewer
writes down what the document is expected to establish. That question becomes
an anchor when the document introduces background information, side issues, or
unfamiliar terminology. A clear anchor also makes it easier to explain why one
paragraph matters more than another. The document does not need to use the
reviewer's exact vocabulary to provide relevant evidence. It does need to make
its claims specific enough that another person can inspect the reasoning.

Evidence often arrives in a different order from the final explanation. An
author may describe a result first and only later introduce the conditions
under which it was observed. A reviewer should keep those conditions attached
to the result. Removing a qualification can make an accurate statement
misleading. Keeping every nearby sentence is not always necessary, but keeping
the sentence that limits the claim is important. This is one reason to inspect
chunk boundaries instead of judging a chunker only by its average chunk size.

A long paragraph can contain several related steps in a single argument. The
first sentence introduces a claim. The next sentence gives an example that
seems to support it. A later sentence identifies a limitation in the example.
The final sentence explains how that limitation changes the original claim.
If the paragraph is too large for one embedding input, the split should prefer
complete sentences. A nearby complete sentence may take the chunk slightly
above its token-size goal. Earlier prose is not repeated in the next chunk.
The original source slices must still reconstruct the document exactly.

The reviewer also distinguishes missing evidence from contradictory evidence.
Missing evidence means that the supplied material does not settle the question.
Contradictory evidence means that at least two observations cannot both support
the proposed interpretation without additional explanation. These situations
need different follow-up actions. One may require another document. The other
may require a revision to the claim. A summary that treats both situations as
the same uncertainty can hide the most important part of the review.

Finally, the reviewer records what would change the decision. This makes the
review useful to someone who was not present for the discussion. A future
reader can compare new information with the stated condition instead of
guessing what the reviewer intended. The result is not a promise that every
future case will look the same. It is a traceable explanation of the current
decision, the evidence that supports it, and the limits that still apply.
